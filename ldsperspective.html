Biblical Inerrancy & the LDS Perspective

Exploring the philosophical, historical, and theological questions surrounding scriptural perfection, prophetic authority, and the nature of faith.

Explore the Analysis

The Core Question

Does the Bible contain errors, or is it perfectly inerrant? The answer depends largely on the standard of perfection being claimed.

Strict Inerrancy

Many evangelical and conservative Christians believe the Bible is completely without error in everything it affirms, both in matters of faith and history. They argue that because God is perfect and inspired Scripture, the original manuscripts were error-free.

Biblical Infallibility

Other Christians believe the Bible is entirely trustworthy for its purpose of revealing God and teaching salvation, but that it may contain minor historical, scientific, or textual imperfections arising from its human authors. This view is common among many mainline Protestant denominations.

Catholic Perspective

The Catholic Church teaches that Scripture teaches "without error that truth which God wanted put into sacred writings for the sake of our salvation." Catholics generally do not insist that every historical or scientific detail must be read as a literal, error-free statement.

Scholarly & Textual Considerations

Many biblical scholars point to variations, discrepancies, and complexities that challenge strict inerrancy.

Examples Often Discussed

Scholars, including some who are Christians and some who are not, point to:

  • Variations between parallel accounts (e.g., differences among Gospel narratives).
  • Numerical discrepancies in certain historical passages.
  • Copying variations among ancient manuscripts.

These scholars often conclude that the Bible reflects both divine inspiration (if viewed through faith) and human authorship, and therefore contains complexities that are difficult to reconcile as strict inerrancy.

Common Examples

  • The differing genealogies of Jesus in Matthew and Luke.
  • Differences in the details of the resurrection accounts among the Gospels.
  • Variations in numbers reported in Kings and Chronicles.

Supporters of inerrancy have proposed explanations for each of these and argue that apparent contradictions can be harmonized.

The Core Question (Revisited)

The debate is not really about evidence alone. It often comes down to prior beliefs about:

  1. Whether God inspired Scripture.
  2. How God would use human authors.
  3. Whether the Bible's purpose is to provide theological truth, precise historical records, or both.

So the honest answer is: If you accept biblical inerrancy, then yes, the Bible is free from error. If you approach it primarily as an ancient collection of historical documents, many scholars conclude it contains discrepancies and human limitations. Reasonable and informed people, including devout Christians, hold both positions.

The LDS Perspective

The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints offers a distinct framework: scripture is highly reliable, but ongoing revelation through living prophets is essential.

Authority ≠ Perfection

The LDS Church does not generally teach that the Bible, as transmitted through centuries, is completely free from error. The eighth Article of Faith states:

"We believe the Bible to be the word of God as far as it is translated correctly."

This creates a different framework:

  • Scripture is highly reliable but not necessarily perfect.
  • Truth can be lost, altered, misunderstood, or interpreted incorrectly over time.
  • Ongoing prophets are needed to clarify doctrine and provide authoritative interpretation.
  • The Great Apostasy explains why sincere Christians arrived at conflicting theological conclusions.

The LDS Reasoning

The LDS reasoning often looks something like this:

  • The Bible teaches important truths.
  • Christians sincerely study the same Bible.
  • Yet they disagree on baptism, salvation, priesthood authority, the nature of God, predestination, sacraments, apostolic succession, and many other doctrines.
  • Therefore Scripture alone appears insufficient to produce doctrinal unity.
  • God resolves this problem by calling living prophets.

This is similar in principle to how ancient Israel frequently had both written scripture and living prophets. The people had the Law of Moses, yet they still needed Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, and others to explain God's will for their own generation.

A Protestant Might Respond

The problem is not Scripture; the problem is human beings misreading Scripture.

An LDS Member Might Reply

If Scripture alone were sufficient, why has it produced thousands of competing interpretations among sincere believers?

The Strongest Criticism

Critics of the LDS position often ask: If prophets are necessary to prevent doctrinal confusion, why have LDS leaders themselves disagreed on some teachings across time? So the philosophical problem is not completely solved. It simply shifts from:

"How do we know our interpretation of Scripture is correct?"

to

"How do we know which prophet is authorized to interpret Scripture?"

At the deepest level, every Christian tradition ends up relying on an authority claim:

  • Protestantism: Scripture is the final authority.
  • Catholicism: Scripture plus the Church's teaching authority (Magisterium).
  • Eastern Orthodoxy: Scripture interpreted through Holy Tradition.
  • LDS: Scripture plus continuing revelation through living prophets.

The LDS view is attractive to many because it offers a mechanism for resolving disputes: living revelation. But it also raises a further question: how does one verify that a particular prophet speaks for God? That question becomes the central issue rather than whether there are discrepancies in the biblical text.

Where the Real Debate Lies

The real dividing question isn't "Are prophets perfect?" Most knowledgeable Latter-day Saints would answer "No." The real question is:

"Can God reliably guide His Church through imperfect people?"

If the answer is yes, then fallibility does not disprove prophetic authority. If the answer is no, then every mistake becomes evidence against prophetic claims. That is ultimately where the LDS perspective and many of its critics diverge. The disagreement is usually not over whether prophets are human. Both sides generally agree they are. The disagreement is whether God can entrust divine authority to imperfect human beings and still accomplish His purposes.

The Four Revealed Works

From an LDS perspective, a significant difference from many forms of Protestantism is the expanded canon. Latter-day Saints claim that God has provided additional sources of revelation:

  1. The Bible
  2. The Book of Mormon
  3. Doctrine and Covenants
  4. Pearl of Great Price
  5. Living prophets and apostles

This creates a larger canon and a continuing mechanism for resolving doctrinal questions. For example:

  • The Bible teaches baptism.
  • The Book of Mormon clarifies the mode and purpose of baptism.
  • Priesthood authority was restored through angelic visitations (John the Baptist for the Aaronic Priesthood; Peter, James, and John for the Melchizedek Priesthood). The Doctrine and Covenants records these events and sets forth the organizational structure and instructions for the Church.
  • Living prophets can address modern questions not directly discussed in ancient scripture.

From an LDS viewpoint, this helps explain why there can be doctrinal certainty despite an imperfect biblical record. The central claim becomes: God did not leave humanity with only an ancient text to interpret. He restored additional scripture and living prophetic authority.

A Protestant Would Likely Reply

Additional scriptures and prophets simply multiply the authorities and create new questions.

An LDS Member Would Respond

Without continuing revelation, Christianity fragmented into thousands of competing interpretations.

The strongest LDS case is not, in my view, "the Bible is perfect." Rather, it is: God historically worked through prophets, apostles, scripture, and continuing revelation. Therefore we should expect the same pattern today. The question then shifts from: "Is the Bible completely error-free?" to "Has God actually restored prophetic authority through Joseph Smith?" If the Restoration is true, then the four standard works together with living prophets provide a framework for resolving doctrinal disputes. If the Restoration is not true, then the existence of additional scriptures doesn't solve the authority problem. Everything ultimately comes back to that foundational claim.

Faith & Works: Reconciling Paul and James

The classic Protestant reconciliation is that Paul and James are talking about different problems, not contradicting each other.

The Protestant Formula

Many Protestants summarize it this way: "We are justified by faith alone, but not by a faith that is alone."

Meaning:

  • Faith saves.
  • Real faith produces works.
  • Works are evidence of salvation, not the cause of salvation.

Paul's Question

How is a sinner declared righteous before God?
Answer: By faith, not by works.

James's Question

What does genuine faith look like?
Answer: Genuine faith produces action.

The Strongest Verses

For sola fide: Romans 3:28 — "Therefore we conclude that a man is justified by faith without the deeds of the law."

Against sola fide: James 2:24 — "Ye see then how that by works a man is justified, and not by faith only." This is the only place in scripture where the phrase "faith only" appears.

Catholics, Orthodox, and many Latter-day Saints often point here and ask: If the Bible teaches faith alone so clearly, why does James literally say "not by faith only"?

The LDS Reconciliation

Latter-day Saints tend to approach the issue differently. An LDS reading often says:

  • Paul is teaching: We cannot save ourselves. Christ's grace is essential.
  • James is teaching: Faith requires action. Covenants and obedience matter.

The Book of Mormon often presents these ideas together: faith in Christ, repentance, baptism, enduring to the end, reliance on grace.

So an LDS interpretation usually sees no need for "faith alone" as a separate doctrine. The question becomes: What kind of faith saves? And the answer would be: A covenantal faith that trusts Christ and follows Him.

A Deeper Observation

What's fascinating is that almost all Christians agree on one thing: Dead faith does not save.

The disagreement is over the role of works.

  • Protestants say: Works reveal saving faith.
  • Catholics, Orthodox, and Latter-day Saints typically say: Works are part of the believer's response to God's grace and therefore participate in the saving relationship.

So the dispute is often less about whether Christians should obey Christ and more about how faith, grace, and obedience relate to justification and salvation. In many ways, Paul and James become much easier to reconcile once you see that they are fighting different errors:

  • Paul fights the idea that we can earn salvation.
  • James fights the idea that salvation requires no transformation.

Faith as Allegiance & Power

Joseph Smith taught that faith is a principle of action and power. Recent scholarship on pistis suggests faith also means allegiance.

Joseph Smith: Faith as a Principle of Action and Power

In the Lectures on Faith, attributed to Joseph Smith and early Church leaders, faith is not merely belief that something is true. Faith is viewed as an active, dynamic principle.

"Faith is the moving cause of all action."

Under this view:

  • Faith causes a person to act.
  • Faith precedes obedience.
  • Faith is a power through which God acts and through which humans access divine power.
  • Miracles, repentance, sacrifice, and covenant keeping all flow from faith.

An LDS reading of James fits very naturally with this. If faith is a principle of action, then "faith without works is dead" is almost self-evidently true. A faith that never acts is not really faith at all.

The "Pistis" / Allegiance View

In recent biblical scholarship, some scholars have argued that the Greek word pistis can mean more than intellectual belief or trust. It can carry ideas of faithfulness, loyalty, fidelity, and allegiance.

Under this framework, when Paul speaks of faith in Christ, he may be describing covenant loyalty to Christ as King rather than mere mental assent. Instead of "Believe these facts about Jesus," the emphasis becomes "Give your loyalty and obedience to Jesus." This interpretation has been advanced by scholars such as Matthew W. Bates and others studying "faith" language in the New Testament.

A Possible Synthesis

You could summarize the LDS view something like this:

Faith is trust in Christ so deep that it produces covenant loyalty, action, sacrifice, obedience, and access to divine power.

That actually combines all three ideas:

  1. Faith as trust.
  2. Faith as allegiance.
  3. Faith as a principle of action and power.

And in that framework, Paul and James become far less difficult to reconcile because neither is describing passive belief. Both are describing a living relationship with Christ that changes how a person acts.

Grace, Effort, and Learning Heaven

Understanding 2 Nephi 25:23 and the true role of grace in the Atonement of Jesus Christ.

2 Nephi 25:23

"For we know that it is by grace that we are saved, after all we can do."

Some interpret this verse to mean that we must earn most of our salvation and then Christ makes up the remaining difference. However, I believe this interpretation misunderstands both grace and the Atonement of Jesus Christ.

No matter how faithfully we serve, repent, sacrifice, obey, or endure, we cannot save ourselves. Even after our best efforts, we remain dependent upon the mercy and merits of Jesus Christ.

"When ye shall have done all those things which are commanded you, say, We are unprofitable servants: we have done that which was our duty to do."
— Luke 17:10

The message of the Restoration is not that we earn salvation. The message is that Jesus Christ has already paid the price we could never pay.

"Jesus doesn't make up the difference. Jesus makes all the difference."
— Brad Wilcox

Grace is not merely something applied at the end of our lives. Grace is the power that forgives us, strengthens us, changes us, and enables us to become more like Christ every day.

The Atonement is not only redeeming. It is enabling. Christ not only cleanses sinners; He transforms disciples.

Our efforts matter, not because they purchase salvation, but because they allow us to receive and respond to the grace God freely offers.

"We are not earning heaven; we are learning heaven."
— Brad Wilcox

Perhaps "after all we can do" means that after all our prayers, all our repentance, all our service, all our covenant keeping, and all our efforts to follow Christ, we still rely completely upon Him.

We do not earn salvation. We learn salvation. We come unto Christ and, through His grace, become new creatures in Him.

Faith and Priesthood Keys

Elder Henry B. Eyring's October 2004 message on seeking a personal witness of priesthood authority.

In October 2004, Elder Henry B. Eyring taught that every disciple should seek a personal witness that priesthood keys reside in Christ's restored Church.

"We have to know by inspiration that the priesthood keys are held by those who lead and serve us."
— Elder Henry B. Eyring, "Faith and Keys"

His message was not that leaders are perfect. His message was that God works through imperfect people who hold divinely appointed authority.
